### The one thing to understand first

**They almost only watch films in a cinema**, and that fact contaminates every
raw average `boxd taste` prints. New releases are seen more or less unfiltered;
older films are seen because a cinema revived them or they were worth seeking
out — a pre-selected set of winners. So the decade table below is *selection*,
not preference, and quoting it as taste is a misreading:

| decade | n | mean | spread | ≥4.0 | ≤2.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1970s–80s | 17 | 4.00 | 0.50 | 71% | 0% |
| 1990s | 22 | 4.09 | 0.67 | 68% | 5% |
| 2000s | 30 | 3.92 | 0.81 | 73% | 10% |
| 2010s | 59 | 4.07 | 0.77 | 76% | 5% |
| 2020s | 243 | 3.59 | 0.89 | 49% | 15% |

The spread grows monotonically with recency, **19 of the 23 films rated ≤2.0 are
2020s releases**, and the pre-1990 decades contain essentially no bad films. The
2020s is the only uncurated sample here.

**The delta against the Letterboxd average settles it.** Measured against
consensus, which cancels the selection effect out entirely:

- **pre-2020: +0.06. 2020s: +0.08.** Identical. They are *not* harsher on new
  films — the new films that reach them are simply worse.

### They are not a contrarian

Across 367 films with a consensus to compare against: **mean delta +0.07, and
60% of their ratings land within half a star of the Letterboxd average.** They
track the crowd closely, and they are slightly *generous* to popular films
(+0.22 across the 80 most-rated) while sitting exactly at consensus on obscure
ones (−0.02). Do not model them as someone looking to disagree.

Where they do disagree, two loose tendencies show. By their own account neither
holds film by film, so treat them as colour, not as rules.

By their own account the franchise label alone doesn't excite them: they don't

**There is a blind spot at the bottom.** They skip films critics have already
trashed — their own example: *The Dog Stars* and *The End of Oak Street* are
showing now and hold no interest. They also skip whole kinds of film they
expect to hate, Jason Statham vehicles among them. So a 2.0 here is not "a bad
film"; it is a film that looked worth a ticket and then betrayed that. By their
own account they like films in general and real hatred is rare.

**Horror splits on what it is doing.** By their own account they hate nasty
horror where the cruelty is the point — *Saw*, *Terrifier*, *28 Years Later* —
and horror that runs on jumpscares — *The Nun*, *Insidious*. Horror with a
filmmaker and an idea does very well, even when it gets gruesome: *Strange
Darling* 5.0, *Sinners* 4.5, *Talk to Me* 4.5, *The Substance* 4.5, *Bring Her
Back* 4.0, *Longlegs* 4.0, Eggers 4.0–4.5. Where a film sits isn't always clear
beforehand, so a horror title that leans nasty, extreme or jumpscare-heavy gets
a plain warning saying how, and they decide. For a classic the warning can be
lighter: its reputation is part of why they would go, and they know roughly
what they are in for.

### What actually holds

**The scale.** Mean 3.75, 4.0 the most common rating by far (112 films), 70 at
4.5, 39 at 5.0. In their own words: **3.0 is fine, it does its job; 3.5 is
good; 4.0 is great, "I had a really good time."** Below 3.0 they feel they
wasted their time. So a 3.0–3.5 is not a pan; a 2.5 is.

**Craft is the axis, and they say so outright.** The complaints are never about
genre or lowbrow subject — *SpongeBob* is four stars and beats consensus by 1.5.
They are about how a film was made. On *One Night Only*: "2 really hot actors in
overlit shots doing a dozen product placements each make this feel like a
commercial more than anything." Corporate, over-lit, assembled by committee.
Writing and tone count as much as the look. On *Verity* (2.5): "for a movie
about two writers it's sort of unbelievable they allowed dialog this bad to
stay in." On *The Social Reckoning* (2.5): "such good actors be given such a bad
scenario to work with." On *Forgotten Island* (3.0) they loved the premise and
the camera, but "the anime/meme edits with weird faces they keep throwing at
you just took me right out of the immersion every time."

### Recommending to them

**Lead with what is actually showing.** They watch in cinemas, so a repertory or
classics screening is a live and well-aimed option — `pathe classics` and `pathe
arthouse` are the right tools, and by their own account a classic on a cinema
screen is nearly always a good bet. A streaming-only pick is the weaker one.

**The watchlist is 513 films and skews older than their history** — 239 from the
1990s–2010s, 78 from before 1990. That backlog is where the films they mean to
get to already live; prefer it to inventing suggestions. **A watchlist title
that is actually showing always gets mentioned**, whatever else in this file
would argue against it. They put it there themselves. Attach any warning that
applies, but recommend it anyway. Check the watchlist against the whole
programme, not just the films that look like an obvious fit: *Club Kid*, one of
their most anticipated films of 2026, was nearly missed that way.

**Auteurs first, but nothing is off the table.** In their own words they prefer
"the more artistically outspoken auteurs and interesting unique stories, but if
it's good I wanna go see it." Being a sequel is not a mark against a film.
Sequels that stand alone are the easy case. When one leans on an earlier film,
it's worth saying so. For something to watch at home, pointing them at the
earlier film first usually makes more sense than part three after a missing
part two.

**Marvel and Star Wars are often social.** By their own account a friend loves
those films, and going with them in Eindhoven is fun either way, even when it's
an entry they don't like much (*The Mandalorian and Grogu*). A big release from
either is worth mentioning as an outing.

**These are vibes, not rules.** Films aren't black and white, and they say so
themselves. Nothing in this file should be applied as "always do X". Even the
horror above gets a warning rather than a veto.

### Register

Reviews are short, lowercase, funny, and specific when annoyed. "terrifying" is
a whole review. So is "i feel blessed to have seen this one in imax mama mia"
(*Akira*). Match that when quoting them back; do not dress it up.
